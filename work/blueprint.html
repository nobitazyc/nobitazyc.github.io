BluePrint Infinite

Bell's customer-service agents used to run two tools side by side on every call: one for the customer's data, one for the step-by-step flow that resolved the call. I led the redesign that merged them into one, built the design system, and shipped the core features in Angular. Average handling time dropped by about a minute per customer.

Role
UX Engineer · design lead
Team
~10 · developers, PM, service managers
Timeline
2019 – 2021

Adobe Analytics
01

Two apps, one call

Context

BluePrint Infinite is the internal tool Bell's customer-service agents use while a customer is on the phone. A flow is a scripted sequence of steps that collects the customer's information and the reason for the call, then walks the agent to a resolution or an offer. Flows are authored by a separate team and cover everything from a bill dispute to booking a technician.

Before the redesign that job was split across two applications. BluePrints Infinite 1.0 showed the customer's profile, services, billing and usage. A second app ran the flows themselves. Agents kept both open and switched between them for the whole length of every call. Every switch cost seconds, broke concentration, and was a chance to lose the thread.

The brief had two parts. Merge the two experiences so an agent can finish a call in one place and in less time. And give the product a real identity, with a design system the team could keep reusing.

02

Ask, then watch

Research

I started with one-on-one interviews with three or four agents and a manager from the service team, walking through their actual day: the calls they get, the workarounds they had built, and the edge cases the previous versions never accounted for.

Then we spent time on the floor of the call centre, watching agents work instead of asking them to describe it. That surfaced habits nobody had mentioned in the interviews. We also visited a Bell retail store, a group with a similar job and different tooling, to see how they handled the same customers and borrow what worked.

03

Four things the redesign had to do

Analysis

The findings went onto a wall with the core developer, the project manager and the customer-service manager, and got boiled down to four problem statements. Every later decision was checked against them.

01

Merge without losing anything

Every function of both apps has to exist in the new one, and be easy to reach.

02

Customer context inside the flow

An agent should be able to browse the customer's information without switching apps or leaving the current step.

03

One step in focus

Hide what isn't needed right now so the agent's attention stays on the current step.

04

Room for steps to grow

A step has to be able to hold more functionality later without redesigning the shell around it.

04

Map what exists

Reverse engineering

Merging two apps means knowing exactly what is in them. I mapped both on a whiteboard: the information architecture of every screen, and the session logic underneath, including the branches for suspended sessions and returning customers that agents relied on but nobody had written down.

05

Draw the system, then the paths

Use cases · User flows

From the whiteboard I built a use-case diagram of the whole system so the team could see how the agent app, the admin tool, the flow engine and the microservices fit together in one view.

Then user flows for the main scenarios in Miro, from the customer dialling in to the flow wrapping up, including the unhappy paths: the customer with one more question, the agent who has to hand off, the flow that has to be cancelled. Having them drawn meant anyone on the team could point at the exact case they were talking about.

06

Settle the shell cheaply

Wireframes

Low-fidelity wireframes in Figma set the structure early: a persistent header with the customer's identity and a flow search, a left navigation, and a content area that changes with the task. They were cheap to change, so the team argued about structure here rather than later on polished mockups.

07

Four problems, four decisions

Hi-fi design

Hi-fi design in Sketch came only after the team had agreed on the structure. Here is how each problem statement turned into something on screen.

Answers 01

One shell for everything

The landing page became a dashboard that carries the whole of the old data app: profile, services and eligibility, usage, timeline and flow history. Predicted and commonly used flows sit right beside them, so the most likely next action is one click away. Flow search lives in the header on every screen, so an agent can start a flow from anywhere.

Answers 02

Customer context beside the step

Inside a flow, an InContext panel keeps account info, profile, usage and tech details alongside the current step, with tabs for the customer's timeline and insights. Sections collapse so the agent chooses what stays open. No more second window.

Answers 03

One step in focus

The flow area shows only the current step. Answers from earlier steps compress into a strip along the bottom, so the agent can see the path and jump back without those steps taking up the screen. Secondary sections of a step start collapsed. Notifications are one bar, not a column. Wrong, Suspend and Cancel are always in the same place.

Answers 04

A step is a container

Each step is a self-contained component with its own state, so a step can be a single question or a full ticket form with a report, an appointment table and flags. I specified the state logic for the developers: what initialises the form, what disables it in read-only mode, and how changes are detected to enable Save. Suspended flows show up as Open Tasks in the sidebar, so an agent can pick up where they left off.

08

A mark for the team, not just the app

Identity · Logo

The second half of the brief was an identity. The old app had a name and a blue header, and nothing else. The team was about to present its work to other departments at Bell and needed something to put on a slide, and the app needed a mark that could survive being shrunk into a sidebar.

I started on paper: letterforms built from the b and the p, peaks for the blueprint idea, and the infinity loop from the product's name. Five made it to vector. Three went onto a sheet where each was checked on white, on dark and on the product blue, because the mark had to live on all three.

The chosen direction became a monogram drawn from circles on a grid so it stays crisp at any size. It sits in the app's sidebar, appears as a watermark behind the dashboard, and its two blues carry through the interface. The mark itself belongs to Bell, so it isn't reproduced here and is masked in the product screens; the exploration that led to it is what's shown.

09

One reference for both sides

Design system

With the shell and the identity settled I built the design system: type scale, colours, spacing, a component library and behaviour rules for the common patterns. Its point was speed for both sides of the table. Designers stop redrawing buttons and tables, developers get one reference to build against, and the back-and-forth between the two shrinks.

10

Designing what I built

Build · Test

I was the UX Engineer on a team of about ten, so the handoff was mostly to myself. The design system became an Angular component library, and the mockups and the app were assembled from the same parts. Where a step's behaviour got complicated I drew the logic before writing it, and the diagrams doubled as documentation for the other developers.

The deadline didn't leave room for a full build before testing. So I pushed the Sketch Cloud prototype as close to the real thing as I could and ran user testing with agents on that. It wasn't perfect, but it gave us real feedback early, and several rounds of polish came out of it before launch.

≈1minless per customer
11

Launched, measured, still going

BluePrint Infinite launched in early 2021, with Adobe Analytics wired in from the first day. The average time an agent spends on one customer dropped by around a minute. Across the call volume of the largest telecom in Canada, that is a lot of hours handed back every week.

The app is still being extended. New features follow the same patterns and come out of the same component library, which was the point of building one.

12

What I'd do differently

Reflection

We never wrote personas or a journey map. The interviews and the time on the floor gave us most of that understanding informally, but writing it down would have made the reasoning visible to people who joined later, and easier to challenge.

Testing on a prototype instead of the build meant some interaction details only surfaced after launch. With more runway I'd have tested on a real build with real customer data, earlier.
